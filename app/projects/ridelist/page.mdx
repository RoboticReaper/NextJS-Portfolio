## Why I built it

I built RideList after experiencing how fragile transportation between Chicago and UIUC can be. A snowstorm delayed my flight, which meant I missed my charter bus. Then I found out that the ticket couldn't be transferred. Trains required inconvenient transfers, and buses often sold out around university breaks. I realized students needed another option.

I'd also seen students informally offer spare seats in their cars, so I wanted to make it easier for student passengers and drivers to find each other. The idea was a scheduled ridesharing marketplace: closer to booking an airline ticket in advance than requesting an on-demand Uber.

## Building and launching RideList

I independently took the product from idea to launch, designing and building the full-stack application with Next.js and PostgreSQL. I implemented ride discovery, booking, real-time messaging, bilingual support, and privacy-preserving geospatial matching with PostGIS. Firebase supports messaging and trip synchronization, while Bash scripts on Linux automate PostgreSQL backups.

I also operated the production infrastructure across Vercel, Google Cloud, and Azure. To secure communication between the Vercel-hosted APIs and my self-hosted database, I set up a custom certificate authority and mutual TLS. The project also uses the Cloudflare API.

Since launch, the platform has supported **more than 60 trips, 75 messages, and seven completed bookings**.

## Finding enough drivers and passengers

The hardest challenge was getting enough people on both sides of the marketplace. Without enough drivers, passengers wouldn't return. Without enough passengers, drivers had little incentive to post rides.

After roughly six months of testing and outreach, I concluded that reaching sustainable scale would require substantially more investment in distribution than I could responsibly make while studying full-time. I transitioned RideList from a startup attempt into a maintained side project.

## What I learned

Shipping the software was only part of building a useful product. Distribution, user behavior, and marketplace dynamics were constraints I had to account for, too. I learned to validate my assumptions honestly, adapt when the evidence changed, and still take ownership of maintaining something real people use.
