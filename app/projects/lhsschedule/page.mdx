The summer before I entered Lexington High School, I heard that our school had an unusual six-day schedule. It repeated every six working days, so the schedule eventually fell out of sync with the days of the week. Holidays and snow days made it even harder to keep track. My friends and I panicked a little because we were worried about going to the wrong class.

I was interested in computer science, and this felt like an opportunity to learn while making something useful for my peers. I decided to build a web app so students could open it on whatever device they had without installing anything. After some research, I chose React for the frontend and Firebase for the backend. Both were new to me, but learning by putting things to use had always worked well for me.

The first problem was figuring out which schedule day it was. I thought about predicting it from the repeating pattern, but holidays, snow days, and unexpected changes could all break that prediction. Then I found a Google Calendar on the school's official website that was regularly updated. Using Chrome's developer tools, I located the calendar's event data in JSON format. That gave me a reliable source I could process and display in my own app.

From there, I used React to show the day's classes and Firebase to let students sign up and save their own class information. They could open their personalized schedule on different devices, verify their email, and reset a forgotten password. Each feature gave me another small challenge to figure out.

I spent entire days watching tutorials, building features, and testing the app. Sometimes I got so frustrated with a problem that I thought about abandoning the project. Imagining my friends actually using it helped me sit down and try again. Eventually, I had a working app that I felt ready to share, so I emailed the student body explaining what it did and why they might find it useful. I remember thinking, "Time to watch my hard work pay off!"

And it did. Every time I checked the registration count, it had gone up by a few, and I'd quietly say "Yes!" with excitement and relief. Soon, there were hundreds of students registered. Emails started coming in with questions, thank-you messages, and suggestions. Those conversations helped me decide what to build next: viewing friends' schedules, using the app offline, and customizing the colors. I also added PDF schedule importing to make entering classes easier.

By the time I finished high school in 2024, 1,400 students had registered, and hundreds were using it daily. Sometimes people recognized me as the creator and told me how convenient it was. I'd started the project because my friends and I were confused about our schedules, and now it was part of other students' everyday routines. I continued maintaining it periodically during my first few months in college, too.

Building this app made me feel closer to my school community. I learned a lot about programming, but I also got to listen to people, respond to their suggestions, and see my work help them. Computer science gave me a voice I could use to make a difference for the people around me.
